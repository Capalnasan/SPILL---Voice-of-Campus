# Spill presenter notes

## Slide 1: Spill

Imagine a student who has received two different exam dates, or a staff member who sees the same administrative misunderstanding every week. The issue may be real, yet the conversation stays in a private chat and never reaches someone who can resolve it. Spill explores a different path: a verified campus community with protected public identities, constructive discussion, and structured feedback for responsible university teams. The project begins in the context of Universitatea Politehnica Timișoara, or UPT. This presentation explains the complete intended system and the working frontend prototype. It does not claim university endorsement or a completed deployment.

## Slide 2: The project in one minute

Spill has two product layers and a necessary institutional partner. The community layer makes participation approachable through posts, replies, reactions, and ordinary campus questions. The feedback layer would organize relevant concerns, support moderation, and prepare reports. University teams would then decide what to do and communicate the outcome. Today, the frontend demonstrates many community interactions with local data. The backend, verified membership, AI assistance, and reporting still need implementation. Response tracking is a proposed extension. Keeping these distinctions clear lets us discuss an ambitious final product while making a realistic assessment of the work already completed and the work ahead.

## Slide 3: Campus experiences worth hearing

A useful feedback channel should accommodate practical problems and useful positive observations. Conflicting announcements may need clarification. Equipment failures may need maintenance. A teaching approach that helped students may be worth repeating elsewhere. These fictional examples are deliberately ordinary because daily friction is a major part of campus experience. They are not allegations about a real university or person. Spill would help identify the nature of a concern and the right route for it. Some questions can be answered by peers. Others require a responsible office. Sensitive matters need a different, more restricted process rather than automatic public discussion.

## Slide 4: Barriers to speaking

The proposal starts with possible barriers rather than a claim that every student feels unable to speak. A person may hesitate because of embarrassment, uncertainty about consequences, or a previous experience of receiving no response. Different groups may face different obstacles, and some may prefer existing channels. A local needs assessment should test which barriers actually matter. Pseudonymity could reduce public exposure, but it does not remove every concern. Clear rules, accessible participation, impartial moderation, and visible follow-up also affect credibility. Spill should be evaluated as a combination of product design and institutional practice, rather than as a privacy setting that automatically creates trust.

## Slide 5: Existing feedback channels

Universities already have important ways to hear their communities. Surveys can organize periodic feedback, representatives can advocate collectively, and support offices can answer individual questions. Formal complaints have authority that a social platform does not possess. Informal chats can be fast and useful, but their conversations may never reach a responsible team. The limitations shown here are possibilities, not ratings of every university's systems. A discovery phase should inventory the actual channels before introducing another one. Spill would be most useful where ongoing discussion, public identity choice, and structured follow-up are currently difficult to combine. In some institutions, improving an existing service may be the better decision.

## Slide 6: The gap Spill aims to address

The opportunity is not simply to create more conversation. It is to make relevant experiences easier to express and easier to connect with someone who can act. A post needs enough context to be understood. Similar concerns may need grouping. A university team needs a clear responsibility, and the contributor needs an understandable update. These are connected requirements. If any link is missing, the platform risks becoming another place where people express frustration without resolution. The proposal therefore includes both a community interface and an institutional workflow. A needs assessment should confirm where existing systems fall short and whether this connected approach would add value.

## Slide 7: Relevance across universities

European quality-assurance guidance provides a context for institutions to consider how they gather and use information about the student experience. That does not mean a specific platform is required. Spill proposes one possible approach to participation and feedback. A large university may need routing across faculties, while a smaller institution may need simpler responsibilities. A commuter community may have different access patterns from a residential campus. The core mechanisms can remain recognizable while categories, verification, staffing, and governance change. The strongest adoption argument is therefore conditional: where a university identifies an unmet need, a carefully governed pilot can test whether Spill is a useful response.

## Slide 8: The people behind the platform

The documented community includes students, teachers, teaching assistants, administrative staff, the Quality Committee, and the Administrative Bureau. These groups do not all need the same permissions. Someone who participates in a discussion should not automatically gain access to moderation records or private identities. Equally, staff should be able to understand and respond to feedback without facing automatic public judgments. Student representatives and independent oversight roles could participate in a governance model, but they are proposals rather than confirmed partners. Designing for multiple perspectives matters because the same platform can feel very different to a student, a lecturer, and an administrator responsible for responding.

## Slide 9: Spill’s proposed value

Each part of the value proposition has a practical mechanism. Membership verification would define who belongs to the community. A pseudonym would separate public participation from the private account record. Discussion and clarification could make a concern more understandable. Moderation and reporting would help responsible people review recurring themes. A response process would explain what happens next. None of these mechanisms alone guarantees a good outcome. Their usefulness must be tested together, including whether people understand the privacy promise and whether the institution can sustain the work. This approach gives the project a more credible foundation than promising that a new app will automatically transform university communication.

## Slide 10: Conversation and institutional feedback

The existing frontend includes broad topics such as campus life, questions, relationships, and small wins. The university feedback concept uses more specific issue categories. A finished product should reconcile these through clear channels. An ordinary study-space question can remain a community conversation. A recurring scheduling problem may become structured feedback. A sensitive allegation may require restricted review, and a grading appeal must retain access to the official process. This separation is a proposed design, not an enforced current feature. It prevents every social post from becoming a complaint and prevents a public conversation from being mistaken for a formal investigation or institutional decision.

## Slide 11: The full user journey

The intended journey begins with membership verification and a clear explanation of public identity. The person then writes a contribution and, where useful, receives suggestions to make it more specific. Review determines the appropriate audience and route. After publication, discussion may add context, while relevant patterns can feed institutional reporting. A proposed status process then explains the response. The current prototype demonstrates substantial parts of composing and conversation. The verification service, shared persistence, AI assistance, institutional reports, and action tracking do not yet form a working end-to-end system. The purpose of this journey is to show how those pieces should eventually fit together without losing the approachable community experience.

## Slide 12: Verified membership

Verification and public identity answer different questions. Verification asks whether someone belongs to the intended university community. Public identity asks how that person appears to other users. Institutional single sign-on could provide the first function without asking Spill to collect a university password directly. Verified institutional email is another possible approach, but it may not always establish current enrollment or employment. The university would need to define eligibility, graduation and departure rules, recovery, and suspension. The prototype currently has frontend authentication screens rather than this integration. A pilot cannot rely on the appearance of a login page as evidence that membership checks and secure sessions are working.

## Slide 13: Pseudonymity and anonymity

Pseudonymity means that a person participates under an app-facing identity rather than their university name. Anonymous posting mode can further hide the public profile association on a particular contribution. The planned backend would still retain a protected relationship to an account for defined safety purposes. This must be explained plainly to users. Text, images, timing, and contextual details can also identify a person even when a name is hidden. The frontend already excludes anonymous posts from the public profile demonstration, but production privacy requires server-side enforcement and careful data access. The project should promise only the protection its architecture and governance can actually provide.

## Slide 17: Designed for everyday access

The current product is a responsive web application, which provides a practical first delivery target across phones and computers. It includes light and dark themes and recorded browser checks at multiple viewport sizes. A released native mobile app is still a future option. Accessibility must cover more than appearance: keyboard operation, visible focus, understandable errors, meaningful labels, contrast, and assistive-technology behavior need review. ## Slide 18: Structured issue categories

The documented taxonomy covers teaching quality, exams and grading, administrative problems, infrastructure issues, safety concerns, and communication problems. Categories should help route an issue rather than label a person as responsible or guilty. Some submissions will span more than one category. For example, conflicting exam dates may be both an assessment and communication concern. A university should adapt the taxonomy to its actual offices and workflows. The social prototype's current topics are not a finalized institutional taxonomy. AI could suggest a category, but the user or reviewer should be able to correct it. Good classification makes the next step easier while preserving the original context.

## Slide 19: AI assistance in the full vision

AI is intended to assist with repetitive language tasks, not to decide whether an allegation is true. It could suggest clearer wording, recommend a category, flag possible risk, and draft summaries for reviewers. The project has not yet deployed this system or measured its accuracy. NIST's AI risk-management work provides a general reference for evaluating reliability and potential harms, rather than evidence that Spill is safe. For this product, evaluation should include missed risks, unfair rejection of legitimate criticism, and distortion of meaning. Users should approve rewrites, and people should review consequential decisions. Provider selection and data handling also need explicit decisions before real content is processed.

## Slide 20: A clearer post, with the same meaning

This example shows a narrow and useful role for AI assistance. The original message expresses frustration but gives little information about what needs attention. The suggested version identifies a concrete inconsistency and a request for clarification. It must not invent dates, people, evidence, or an explanation for why the problem occurred. It also must not soften the criticism until its meaning disappears. The author should review the suggestion, edit it, or keep their own wording. ## Slide 21: Moderation and human oversight

Moderation needs to consider the purpose of the channel and the possible consequences of publication. An ordinary campus question may be straightforward. A vague issue may benefit from clarification. A sensitive accusation or identifying detail may require restricted review. A serious risk may need escalation beyond the platform. These routes should not turn moderation into censorship of uncomfortable criticism. Reviewers need clear policies, decision reasons, and a way to correct mistakes through appeals. Their normal work should not require learning an anonymous author's identity. The current moderation screen demonstrates interactions using sample reports. A production service requires permissions, persistence, trained people, and an auditable workflow.

## Slide 22: Community safety controls

Users need immediate ways to manage their experience and a reliable way to raise concerns. The frontend includes report, mute, block, and moderation-related interactions, while the roadmap identifies remaining refinements such as report reasons and reversible preferences. These controls become meaningful at university scale only when the server enforces them consistently. Spam limits, upload restrictions, role checks, and retained moderation decisions also need implementation. Draft rules provide a starting point, but they require review and operational support. Safety should therefore be described as a system of product controls, human processes, and technical enforcement. A visible button or draft policy page is not sufficient proof that the underlying protection is complete.

## Slide 23: Sensitive cases need different handling

Consider a fictional person who says that an interaction during a campus activity made them feel unsafe. The right response may be to help them reach a confidential support or reporting route, not to publish identifying allegations immediately. Reviewers should distinguish urgent help from the ordinary moderation queue. The platform must show university-approved emergency and support information, with clear availability limits. Neither an AI label nor crowd reaction determines guilt. Exceptional identity access is a separate decision with its own authority and safeguards. The proposed escalation design needs institution-specific review before a pilot, especially for situations where delays or disclosure could increase risk.

## Slide 24: Three-person identity approval

The project calls this the three-key reveal mechanism. Its documented meaning is three separate responsible people approving an exceptional identity disclosure, with a reason and audit record. It does not establish cryptographic key splitting or a finished security implementation. A real design needs independent roles, conflict-of-interest rules, narrow access, and oversight of requests and denials. Three approvals alone do not make a request appropriate or lawful. Applicable authority and obligations still require review. Any exceptional legal route must be governed explicitly rather than silently bypassing the public promise. This safeguard should be rare and understandable, while ordinary moderation continues without exposing an author's private identity.

## Slide 25: Privacy and trust by design

Privacy needs to shape the information architecture before real university data enters the system. The EDPB's guidance on data protection by design and by default provides a relevant general reference. For Spill, the proposal is to separate public content from protected identity references, limit who can access each, and avoid collecting information simply because it might be interesting. Reports also need review: a detailed quotation from a very small group can expose someone even when their name is removed. Retention and deletion need clear rules. These are requirements for future implementation and institutional assessment. The current browser-based prototype is not a secure identity vault or a completed privacy program.

AI could draft sections, but a person should check that the summary remains faithful to the underlying content and does not expose identities. ## Slide 28: Closing the feedback loop

QAA's guidance on student partnership emphasizes meaningful participation and communicating how student input affects decisions. Spill's proposed response loop applies that principle to a continuous feedback channel. The institution would acknowledge relevant issues, assign responsibility, provide updates, and explain an outcome. Sometimes the outcome may be that a change is not feasible or that a formal route is required. A clear explanation can still make the process more understandable. This is a proposed extension, not an automatic consequence of collecting posts. The product needs status information, but the institution needs people and authority to keep that information meaningful. Without follow-through, additional feedback collection may disappoint users.

For a student, the first benefit may be simple: knowing where to ask a question or describe an experience. ## Slide 31: Value for teaching and administrative staff

Staff may benefit when feedback becomes specific enough to respond to. A lecturer can understand which part of an instruction caused confusion, while an administrative team can identify a document checklist that needs updating. Positive observations can also show which practices students find useful. However, this value depends on fair handling. A platform must not turn isolated comments into staff performance scores or treat popularity as an evaluation method. Staff need a proportionate opportunity to add context through the appropriate channel. Moderation independence, confidentiality, and workload planning matter here. The pilot should ask staff whether the information helps them act, rather than assume that more incoming feedback is automatically beneficial.

## Slide 32: Value for institutional decision-makers

Leadership may find value in seeing recurring concerns in a more organized form. That information can support conversations across offices and make it easier to explain decisions to the community. It does not replace established evidence, professional judgment, or formal quality processes. More visible reports may initially increase because people find the channel easier to use, not because campus conditions suddenly worsen. The institution also takes on operational responsibilities: reviewing reports, assigning issues, handling sensitive cases, and communicating progress. A pilot should therefore evaluate decision usefulness and workload together. The goal is better understanding and accountable response, not simply a larger count of posts or an attractive dashboard.

## Slide 33: Adapting Spill to each university

The UPT context provides the initial setting, but a broader platform would need to respect institutional differences. Universities may have different identity providers, student populations, committee structures, and support services. They may also use different languages and reporting categories. A shared product can offer common interaction patterns while each university defines the operational rules that make those patterns meaningful. Multi-university operation additionally requires strong separation of data and permissions. One university should not browse another's raw submissions or private identities. Cross-campus analysis would require its own purpose and review. Expansion should follow evidence that the product and the operating model work in a bounded setting.

## Slide 34: How Spill compares with alternatives

The comparison is about common channel types, not current feature claims about named products. Some universities already combine discussion, ticketing, surveys, and reporting effectively. Others may have separate systems that do not connect well. Spill's proposed differentiation is the integration of a pseudonymous community experience with structured feedback and an institutional response route. That does not give it the authority of a formal complaint system. A pilot assessment should include integration possibilities and the option of improving an existing platform. The strongest case for adoption is a demonstrated local gap and a workable operating model, rather than the assertion that every alternative lacks the same features.
